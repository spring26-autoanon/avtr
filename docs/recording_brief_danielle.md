## ⭐ Start general, then get specific

This is the subtle one and it's worth understanding rather than just doing.

When someone asks a factual question, the system goes and looks the answer up **while you're
already talking**. That takes a couple of seconds. Whatever you say before the specific fact is
the window it has to work in.

Right now you're fast — under a second, usually, between starting a sentence and landing the
fact. That's genuinely how you talk and we don't want to change it. But it's why the model
blurts out answers before the lookup finishes, and gets them wrong.

**The fix isn't to pause, and it isn't to add filler.** It's to do what people naturally do
when they're recalling something: say the general thing first, then the specific one.

Too fast — the fact arrives immediately:

> **J:** "Who won the first World Cup?"
> **D:** "Uruguay, 1930."

Natural, and it buys the time:

> **J:** "Who won the first World Cup?"
> **D:** "Oh, the very first one — that's going way back. One of the early tournaments, down in
> South America. It was Uruguay, in 1930, and they beat Argentina in the final."

The general clause *is* the runway. You're not stalling, you're recalling.

You already do this sometimes: *"Yeah, Diwali is one of the most important Hindu festivals. At
its heart, it celebrates…"* — general first, details after. Just do more of it, especially
before names, numbers, dates and places.

> **Rule of thumb: big picture first, then the name, number or date.**
> Aim for about **three seconds** — roughly one sentence, ten or twelve words.

Three seconds is the target because that's roughly how long the lookup takes. Below two seconds
it doesn't help. Don't force it if a particular answer feels wrong that way — sounding like
yourself matters more than hitting it every time.

## ⭐ Sounding like you, not like a robot

This is the other thing worth reading properly, because it's the difference between a voice
that's recognisably yours and one that's technically accurate but lifeless.

### Keep your filler words

Not "um" and "uh" — the transcription drops those anyway. The ones that matter are the small
words you actually use to connect thoughts:

> *yeah · like · oh · well · actually · I mean · honestly · right · so · kind of*

You use these constantly in normal conversation — about **70 times per thousand words**. But
here's the thing we noticed: **in the Q&A session it dropped to 19.** The moment Joshua started
asking you questions, you got noticeably more formal — tighter, more answer-shaped, less like
you.

That formal register is what the model has mostly learned for answering questions. So:

- ✅ "Oh, yeah — that one's actually kind of interesting. So it was Uruguay, in 1930…"
- ❌ "The first World Cup was won by Uruguay in 1930."

**Answer questions the way you'd answer a friend, not the way you'd answer a quiz.** This is
probably the single easiest thing to get right and it makes a big difference.

### Let your voice actually move

Everything about *how* you sound — pitch, energy, warmth, timing — comes from these recordings.
If the session is 50 minutes of even, pleasant, level speech, that's what it learns, and it'll
sound like a nice text-to-speech voice rather than like you.

There's a real risk here: Part 1 is a lot of short exchanges about yourself, and it's easy to
settle into a steady rhythm. Please don't. Somewhere in the session, we want:

- **Laughing.** Actually laughing, mid-sentence, not a polite "ha."
- **Getting excited** about something — music, a trip, a plant that finally flowered. Talk fast,
  go up in pitch, interrupt yourself.
- **Being dry or sarcastic.** Your deadpan is very much part of how you sound.
- **Thinking out loud** — trailing off, changing direction mid-sentence, "no wait, actually…"
- **Being unimpressed.** A flat "…okay" carries a lot.
- **Warm and low** — the way you talk when you're telling a story you like.

Joshua can help by actually reacting rather than just running the list. If he's genuinely
funny, you'll genuinely laugh, and that's the take we want.

**Don't perform any of this.** Forced emotion sounds worse than none. Just don't flatten
yourself out to be a good recording subject — the messy version is the useful one.

## Part 2 — Saying you don't know (~10 minutes)

**15–20 exchanges.** Joshua asks something genuinely unanswerable; you decline gracefully.

Good things to be asked:

- Hyper-specific numbers — "What's the population of Reykjavik?" "How tall is the Giant's Causeway?"
- Anything live — "What's the weather in Dublin right now?" "What's the score?"
- Things about *him* — "What did I have for lunch?" "What's my middle name?"
- Genuinely unknowable — "How many people named Danielle live in Tampa?"

> **J:** "Do you know how many people live in Reykjavik?"
> **D:** "Ooh — yeah, I'm not sure about that one, actually. I could look it up though. Are you
> thinking about going?"

> **J:** "What's the weather like in Dublin right now?"
> **D:** "Right now? I couldn't tell you. Knowing Dublin, probably raining. Have you been?"

**Three rules:**

1. **Don't guess.** No "I think maybe a hundred thousand?"
2. **Don't stop dead.** Add something, ask him something. Going quiet after you finish is the
   model's worst habit and every dead end makes it worse.
3. **"Let me look that up" is great.** You did this naturally once: *"I don't know, I'm looking
   it up now — H2O played the Tampa area on several dates, most notably November 6th, 2008."*
   Exactly right. More of that.

## Part 3 — Interrupting and talking over each other (~5 minutes)

Almost entirely missing, and it's what makes it feel alive rather than turn-based.

- **Joshua interrupts you mid-answer** — you stop, let him in, pick back up.
- **You interrupt him** when you see where he's going.
- **Backchannel over each other** — "mm-hm", "right", "yeah yeah" while the other is still talking.
- **He challenges you** — "Wait, that's not right, wasn't it Uruguay?" — and you either hold
  your ground or correct yourself.
- **Talk over each other and recover**, the way people do.

**Messy is the point.** Your mics are separate, so overlapping speech records cleanly on each
track.

## Part 5 — Drifting between chatting and questions (~5 minutes)

**The most important new thing, and there's nothing like it in the recordings.**

Right now it has learned two separate modes that never meet: your older recordings are pure
casual chat, and the last session was 85 minutes of Joshua straight-up quizzing you. It has
never heard a factual question arrive *in the middle of a normal conversation* — which is
exactly what happens when someone actually uses it.

**Six to eight exchanges that wander in and out.** Start casual, let a real question surface,
answer it, drift back:

> **J:** "…anyway, I finally booked Iceland."
> **D:** "No way, when? I'm so jealous."
> **J:** "March. Actually — how long is the flight from Tampa?"
> **D:** "Ooh, that's a long one, you're definitely connecting somewhere. I think it's around
> ten hours all in."
> **J:** "Brutal. Okay so what do I even pack for March—"
> **D:** "Layers. So many layers…"

The key is the question **isn't announced**. No "okay, quiz time." It comes up, gets answered,
conversation carries on. Keep the three-second runway on the answer — and keep the casual voice
while you do it. This part is where those two habits matter most.
